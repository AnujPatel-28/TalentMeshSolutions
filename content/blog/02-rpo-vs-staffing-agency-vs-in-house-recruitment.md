## The question behind the question

"Should we hire a recruiter or use an agency?" is usually asked when something has already gone wrong — a critical role has been open for two months, or the founder is spending a third of the week on interviews.

The honest answer depends on three things you can measure today: how many people you will hire in the next twelve months, how predictable that number is, and how specialised the roles are. Get those three right and the model chooses itself.

There are three broad options, and most growing companies eventually use some combination of all three.

## Model 1: In-house recruiting

You employ recruiters directly. They sit in your team, learn your product, and own the funnel end to end.

**Where it wins**

- **Employer brand.** An internal recruiter represents you naturally because they are you. Over time they build a candidate network that stays with the company.
- **Cost per hire at steady volume.** Once you are hiring consistently, a salaried recruiter is cheaper per hire than paying a percentage on every placement.
- **Institutional memory.** They remember the candidate who was strong but joined elsewhere, and can call them a year later.
- **Control.** Priorities shift the same day you shift them.

**Where it struggles**

- **Fixed cost against variable demand.** A recruiter is a full-time salary whether you have fifteen roles open or none. Hiring rarely arrives at a steady rate.
- **Bench depth.** One recruiter is a single point of failure. When they take leave, hiring stops.
- **Niche reach.** An internal recruiter builds depth in your usual roles. The moment you need a skill you have never hired before, they start from zero.
- **Ramp time.** Recruiting a recruiter takes time, and they need months to become productive.

**Fits when:** you hire steadily and predictably, most roles are variations on ones you have filled before, and employer brand is a real competitive lever for you.

## Model 2: Staffing or recruitment agency

You engage an external partner per role or per set of roles, usually on a contingency basis — you pay when a candidate joins.

**Where it wins**

- **Cost follows outcome.** No hire, no fee. Nothing sits on your fixed cost base.
- **Immediate reach.** A specialist agency already knows the market for the role and often knows the candidates.
- **Elasticity.** Three roles this quarter, twelve the next — no hiring plan of your own required.
- **Speed on the roles that hurt.** For a hard-to-fill position, an agency that has placed it repeatedly will beat a generalist internal recruiter almost every time.
- **Confidentiality.** Replacing an incumbent, or hiring for a business line you have not announced, is far easier through a third party.

**Where it struggles**

- **Per-hire cost.** At high volume, percentage fees add up faster than a salary would.
- **Variable depth of engagement.** An agency working ten mandates gives yours a share of attention. Quality depends heavily on the individual recruiter, not the logo.
- **Brand distance.** Candidates hear about you second-hand unless the agency is properly briefed.
- **Knowledge does not compound in-house.** What the agency learns about your market largely stays with the agency.

**Fits when:** hiring is spiky, roles are specialised or senior, or you need a search kept quiet. Also the right first step for any company hiring fewer than roughly a dozen people a year — a full-time recruiter cannot be justified at that volume.

## Model 3: Recruitment process outsourcing (RPO)

An external team runs some or all of your recruitment function as an extension of your own — usually on a retained or per-hire commercial model, with defined process ownership, service levels and reporting.

The distinction from a staffing agency matters. An agency fills roles you hand it. An RPO partner owns a process: intake, sourcing, screening, interview coordination, offer management, sometimes onboarding — and reports on funnel metrics the way an internal team would.

**Where it wins**

- **Scale without headcount.** Hiring two hundred people for a new site does not require you to hire twenty recruiters and then let them go.
- **Process discipline.** Structured intake, consistent screening, real funnel data — usually a step up from ad-hoc internal hiring.
- **Predictable economics.** Costs are agreed in advance rather than discovered role by role.
- **Coverage across role types.** One arrangement can span volume, specialist and leadership hiring.
- **Flexible scope.** You can outsource only sourcing and screening while keeping decisions, or hand over the whole funnel.

**Where it struggles**

- **Commitment.** RPO is a contracted relationship, not a per-role transaction. Getting out of a bad one is slower.
- **Onboarding investment.** The partner needs real access to your managers and your context. Companies that treat RPO as a black box get black-box results.
- **Overkill at low volume.** Below a certain hiring rate, the structure costs more than it saves.

**Fits when:** you are hiring at volume or expanding into a new location, hiring is chronically inconsistent across teams, or you need recruiting capability quickly without adding permanent headcount.

## A decision framework

Work through these in order.

**Step 1 — Count the hires.** Roughly how many people will you hire in the next twelve months?

- Under 10: agency, engaged per role.
- 10 to 40: one internal recruiter for repeat roles, plus an agency for the specialised and senior ones.
- Over 40, or concentrated in bursts: RPO for the bulk, with internal or search support for leadership. If a large share of that number lands in a single drive, the model question is only half the problem — see our [bulk hiring playbook](/blog/bulk-hiring-in-india-a-step-by-step-playbook) for how to run the drive itself.

**Step 2 — Test predictability.** If your twelve-month number could plausibly be half or double what you planned, avoid loading it onto fixed headcount. Variable demand belongs with a variable-cost model.

**Step 3 — Sort roles by scarcity.** List your open roles and mark each as *repeatable* (you have hired it before and can describe a good candidate precisely) or *scarce* (small candidate pool, or a skill new to you). Repeatable roles suit in-house or RPO. Scarce roles suit specialist search, whoever runs the rest.

**Step 4 — Be honest about manager bandwidth.** Every model fails if hiring managers do not show up. If interview feedback routinely takes a week, no sourcing engine will save your time-to-hire. Fix the internal loop first, or choose a partner who will actively chase the process.

**Step 5 — Decide what must stay inside.** Offer negotiation, final decisions and candidate experience at the closing stage are worth keeping close regardless of model.

## The combination most growing companies land on

In practice the answer is rarely one model:

- An internal recruiter or HR generalist owning repeat roles and employer brand
- A staffing partner for specialist, senior and confidential searches
- An RPO arrangement switched on for expansions, new sites and volume drives

The important thing is that the choice is deliberate. Most companies drift into a model by accident and then blame the model.
